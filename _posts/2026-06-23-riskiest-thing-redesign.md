Everything search engines learned about the old one is attached to URLs you're about to change.

I've run migrations on sites with millions of monthly visitors. Traffic survival comes down to a spreadsheet almost nobody wants to own: every old URL, where it now points, and what happens to the ones with no clean match.

That last group is where traffic dies. A retired page with no real equivalent gets pointed at the homepage as a lazy default, search engines read it as a dead end, and the rankings that page earned over years are gone. Multiply that across a few hundred URLs and you've undone work no one can see you losing.

The matching is getting automated now. Tools can embed both versions of a site and suggest the closest content match for every dead URL. That helps. It also moves the failure mode. It's easy to bulk-approve 300 "closest matches" that are 95% similar and still wrong, because the page about your Cleveland branch got mapped to the one about Columbus.

So the real work is judgment. Redirect matches someone actually checked. Structured data rebuilt template by template so the rich results don't vanish. Crawling the staging site the way a search engine would, before launch.

A migration that keeps its rankings looks like nothing happened. Same traffic, new site, no drama. That quiet is the whole deliverable.

If your company has a redesign coming, ask one question early: who owns the redirect map? If the answer is "the agency, probably," your traffic is the thing at risk.

Nearly 400 people showed up to the Greater Cleveland Partnership AI Summit a couple weeks ago to hear that AI has moved from experimentation to execution.

I agree but would add that execution is where the work begins and where the demos stop being useful.

![A robot figure bound in chains or restraints, representing AI governance and constraint](/assets/images/shackled-robot.jpg)

I run web content for an organization with 11 branches and seasonal programs that change constantly. AI can draft that content fast but speed was never the issue. The fast version was recognizably AI. It sounded generic and a little inflated. Smooth in a way that real staff never are. For a community organization, that tone quietly costs you trust. People feel it before they can name it.

So I didn't hand the work to a tool and walk away. I wrote a governing workflow first. A source hierarchy, so every claim traces back to an actual program guide. Hard rules against marketing filler and the usual AI tells. A required audit step where the tool flags its own violations and rewrites before a human ever sees it.

The drafting was fast. Writing the rules to govern it took longer.

That's the gap I keep noticing. Summits sell what AI can do. The teams getting real value have decided, on paper, what it isn't allowed to do.

A question for anyone deploying this: who in your org actually owns the guardrails?
